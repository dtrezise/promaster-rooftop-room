The product vision

A second room—
without a second vehicle.

The concept treats the van roof as a complete living-space platform, not simply a place to attach a conventional sleeping tent.

01

Use the whole roof

A full-length cassette turns the available roof footprint into a large, continuous room rather than a compact two-person sleeping shell.

02

Stand naturally

The target is genuine standing height throughout the central room—not the 39–48 inch interior common to rooftop tents.

03

Close the weather out

Insulated sandwich panels, thermally broken joints, compression seals and controlled ventilation define the four-season direction.

04

Let the structure deploy itself

Air-assisted motion and synchronized actuators perform the work; rigid posts, beams and mechanical locks carry the final loads.

Recommended architecture

Pneumatic origami.
Mechanical certainty.

Inflation is used as muscle and seal—not as the sole structure. Hard panels move in a controlled sequence, meet at rigid frames and lock before occupancy.

  1. 1

    Lift

    Four synchronized telescoping posts raise the rigid roof shell to standing height.

  2. 2

    Unfold

    Segmented insulated wall leaves rotate into place with pneumatic hinge assistance.

  3. 3

    Lock

    Over-center latches and structural mullions establish the occupied wind and snow load paths.

  4. 4

    Seal

    Inflatable perimeter gaskets preload every panel joint against rain, drafts and thermal leakage.

The governing rule

Air moves it.
Mechanics hold it.

The credible first product

Full-roof footprint.
Moderate expansion.

A roughly 13 × 6 foot room already provides about 78 square feet. Keeping the first version near roof width sharply reduces panel area, mass, wind load and automatic-folding complexity.

  • Full-length aerodynamic travel cassette
  • Three or four structural bays per long wall
  • Two folding leaves through the wall height
  • Optional 12–18 inch lateral floor extensions
  • External access for the first no-drill prototype

Feasibility verdict

Conditionally feasible.
Not yet fabrication-ready.

The experience is coherent and the mechanism is credible. Vehicle loads, mass, panel joints, automatic sequencing and weather performance still have to be proved physically.

The defining constraint

Hard walls change the mass equation.

A roof-footprint room contains roughly 344 square feet of walls and roof before counting the floor. Even an aggressive 1.5 lb/ft² panel assumption produces about 516 pounds of enclosure skin before frames, posts, actuators, rack and interior equipment. The earlier 400–450 pound soft-wall target must therefore be re-baselined.

Development roadmap

Prove one bay.
Then prove the room.

The fastest credible path is a sequence of increasingly representative tests—not a complete rooftop build on the first attempt.

  1. A

    Kinematic study

    Quarter-scale folding model

    Resolve wall segmentation, hinge order, interference and stowed thickness with inexpensive physical models.

  2. B

    Mechanism proof

    Full-scale single structural bay

    Test a representative roof post, two wall leaves, air assist, locks, seals and obstruction detection.

  3. C

    Vehicle proof

    No-drill full-length cassette

    Measure travel mass, mount reactions, road behavior, water management and emergency stow before occupancy.

  4. D

    Environment proof

    Occupied hard-wall prototype

    Run structural, wind, weather, thermal, condensation and life-cycle testing under a controlled operating envelope.

The opportunity

Not a rooftop tent.
A new room for the van.

The concept is ambitious because it combines the space of an integrated pop-top with a low-profile travel form and hands-free deployment. The opportunity is real—but only a disciplined prototype program can turn the vision into a safe product.
